- HBR Guide to Performance Management
	- performance is made up of two factors: behaviours and results. results = "what" of the performance. behaviours = "how"
	- goal setting includes agreeing on specifc targets, how progress will be evaulted, how to measure results, how to guage behavioral expectations. as manager, should work directly with employees to set these goals
		- managers need to understand which characteristics effective goals have, where to draw potential targets from, and how challenging to make the objectives
		- attributes of well-defined goals:
			- (1) aligned with the work that matters most to the company and respects ppl's time, energy, and resources
				- set up a performance-planning session. if possible, schedule a separate sesh w/ each employee.
					- be prepared to adjust those targets before the review period ends. let them know that another meeting might be in order
					- ask employee to list some goals before meeting. give points about what makes an effective goal and review some promising sources for coming up with possible targets
				- define goals that account for both the organizational needs and the employee's professional aspirations
					- aligning with organization's goals: discuss the efforts of each person's energy to make the company work and achieve it's larger strategic objectives. understanding *why* it's important will make it more meaningful. discuss how their goals align. mention any missing goals
					- aligning personal interests with professional goals: ask them if they would be comfy sharing their personal interests during a performance-planning meeting if their drafted list doesnt include any. consider adjusting their goals to include those interests.
				- set a reasonable number of goals & prioritize 2-4 challenging, specific, significant goals
					- these goals can be shorter-term with monthly or quarterly targets.
					- these goals can also be smaller parts of complex/large/overwhelming (but not necessarily unachievable) goals
				- establish a way to measure success: determine how to assess progress toward each goal and list anticipated outcomes and measures for each item.
					- common pitfalls:
						- missing metrics: might not know the starting point to measure a 5% increase in sales or something. similarly just setting "increase sales" as a goal is unclear
						- choosing the wrong metrics: not all measurements are equally important or worth targeting. eg: reduce number of complaints overall > increase number of customer complaints resolved in a given time period
						- overemphasizing metrics: recognizing and acknowledging the behaviour and work behind a specific goal is just as important as the metric itself. eg: helping others in the workplace or coaching new employees might not be part of anyone's formal goals, but they are important.
					- difficult to quantify
			- (2) specific and measurable to determine when the goal has been reached
			- (3) framed in time, with clear deadlines
			- (4) stretches individuals while still being achievable
			- (5) geared toward improving current performance & future growth
			- (6) tailored to the individual by involving them in the process & creating a sense of ownership/commitment
			- (7) documented and kept in the front of the mind to promote regular assessment of progress and avoid forgetting
		- sources of goals:
			- organization, division, or department plans and strategies
			- goals from previous review periods or those that are linked to critical job responibilities (evergreen goals)
			- comments from prev performance reviews and feedback discussions
